# APRA CPS 234: what a board should ask about security

On 10 June 2025, the Australian Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA)—the independent government body that supervises Australian banks, insurers, and superannuation funds—sent an urgent letter to the board chair of every super trustee in Australia. Online scammers using stolen passwords had exposed weak login controls across member portals. APRA instructed each trustee to review its cyber security protections thoroughly by 31 August 2025 and nominate a senior executive personally accountable for meeting APRA's mandatory security standard, known as Prudential Standard CPS 234 ([APRA](https://www.apra.gov.au/news-and-publications/action-information-security-obligations-and-critical-authentication-controls), June 2025).

Notice that the regulator's letter went straight to the board chair, not to the IT department or external computer contractors. Under Australian prudential law, the board of directors carries ultimate legal responsibility for protecting customer records, banking systems, and company data ([APRA, CPS 234](https://www.apra.gov.au/standards/cps-234)). 

Many Australian board directors and committee members are seasoned business leaders, lawyers, or community representatives who do not come from a computer engineering background. When cyber security appears on the board pack, it is easy to feel overwhelmed by technical acronyms. Here is a clear, straightforward guide to the exact questions directors need to ask executive management, what a genuinely reassuring answer sounds like, and the concrete records you should expect to see on the table.

## What APRA CPS 234 puts on the board

Prudential Standard CPS 234 has been enforceable Australian law since 1 July 2019. It binds all authorised deposit-taking institutions (ADIs, which include retail banks, building societies, and credit unions), general and life insurers, friendly societies, private health insurers, and superannuation trustees. For a local super fund, "the board" can mean a collective group of individual trustees. For a foreign bank operating an Australian branch, it means the designated senior officer in Australia.

The core legal obligation is simple: the board must ensure that the organisation maintains information security controls that match the size and nature of threats to its digital assets ([APRA, CPS 234](https://www.apra.gov.au/standards/cps-234)).

In everyday language, an "information asset" means far more than just the main banking database or accounting software. APRA defines it as all electronic information, computer hardware, desktop software, customer records, spreadsheets stored on staff laptops, and even physical paper files kept in filing cabinets.

Importantly, the prudential standard does not bind technology vendors directly. It binds the licensed institution itself. The board remains legally accountable for customer data even when that data is hosted, processed, or managed by external cloud software companies. The institution must classify every asset, test external vendors' security capabilities, review their safeguard design, and enforce strict reporting clauses in contracts. If a group parent company operates in Australia, the board must also ensure these protections extend across all subsidiaries, including non-regulated business arms.

CPS 234 operates hand-in-hand with APRA's operational resilience standard, CPS 230. Where CPS 234 focuses on keeping sensitive information private and defended against hackers, CPS 230 focuses on ensuring essential banking services keep running during an outage. Our companion guide on [what CPS 230 expects of technology vendors](/blog/cps-230-technology-vendors) explains that operational side in detail.

## What APRA found in board reporting

APRA has been remarkably candid about weaknesses in Australian boardrooms. In late 2021, the regulator reported that it saw very little evidence of directors actively testing, questioning, or challenging the cyber security updates presented by management. Board packs were frequently cluttered with technical jargon that obscured real operational risks, and several boards received no clear proof whether their internal security tests had actually worked ([APRA](https://www.apra.gov.au/news-and-publications/improving-cyber-resilience-role-boards-have-play), November 2021).

The regulator made its goal clear: company directors must feel just as confident questioning cyber security and IT defenses as they do reviewing monthly financial accounts, audited balance sheets, or annual budgets.

Over subsequent years, APRA carried out detailed independent reviews across the industry:

| Date | Regulatory action taken by APRA |
|---|---|
| July 2023 | Released initial findings from independent CPS 234 cyber audits covering more than 300 Australian financial institutions. |
| June 2024 | Issued formal guidance requiring entities to verify the reliability and physical isolation of data backups. |
| August 2024 | Warned boards regarding software configuration errors, unmonitored privileged accounts, and superficial security testing. |
| June 2025 | Issued formal directives to all superannuation chairs demanding multi-factor login protections across member accounts. |
| August 2026 | Mandated deeper board oversight of emerging artificial intelligence (AI) risks and post-quantum cryptography in its corporate plan. |

Those comprehensive supervisory reviews revealed five persistent blind spots across Australian organisations ([APRA](https://www.apra.gov.au/news-and-publications/cyber-security-stocktake-exposes-gaps), July 2023):

- Incomplete lists and informal classifications of which customer records and databases were genuinely critical or sensitive
- Inadequate background checks and shallow technical vetting of third-party software vendors
- Annual security testing routines that were poorly scoped or carried out haphazardly
- Limited independent scrutiny by internal audit teams of day-to-day security safeguards
- Slow or inconsistent notifications to APRA when major incidents or material control breakdowns occurred

## Questions about assets and third parties

### Which information assets matter most?

Every board discussion should begin with the company's asset register. A direct, practical question to ask management is: *"Which specific databases and software applications are classified as critical or sensitive, and who made that determination?"* 

Under CPS 234, both ratings have precise meanings. "Criticality" measures the business damage if a system stops working or goes offline (loss of availability). "Sensitivity" measures the harm to customers and the organisation if records are stolen, leaked, or maliciously altered (loss of confidentiality or integrity).

A solid answer will point to an approved company-wide methodology and a freshly updated register listing every repository, including systems managed by external software providers. In APRA's stocktake, many third-party systems holding sensitive customer data were missing from registers altogether. If your management team's list stops at your own building's front door, the board is only seeing half the picture.

### How do we know a vendor's controls work?

Verifying external suppliers is often the most challenging duty for a board. When customer records are held on an external cloud server, the safeguards are physically managed by somebody else. In APRA's 2021 review, 60 per cent of financial entities had never independently reviewed the security testing conducted by their key technology suppliers. Many simply relied on polite vendor questionnaires or self-completed tick-box forms without verifying the claims.

Consider an everyday scenario: an Australian credit union or regional insurer uses a modern cloud platform to handle customer applications. Every twelve months, the vendor emails across a marketing brochure and an annual security certificate. While comforting, the board must ensure that the vendor's actual testing rigour matches the true sensitivity of the customer records involved. If a security failure at the vendor could cause severe financial harm or distress to policyholders, the organisation's internal audit team must independently review the vendor's evidence.

The crucial question for directors is: *"For our highest-risk external suppliers holding customer files, what recent, independent proof of testing have we examined?"* 

A commercial certificate is only a starting point. Our detailed analysis of [what a SOC 2 report does and does not prove](/blog/soc-2-for-buyers) explains how to read vendor audit scopes carefully. APRA also identified supplier contracts that lacked mandatory clauses requiring the vendor to notify the institution immediately during a cyber breach. Without that contract clause, meeting APRA's 72-hour reporting rule becomes nearly impossible.

## Testing that tells the board something

APRA recommends that directors ask management a fundamental question: *"What proportion of our security controls undergo regular, independent testing?"* 

While that is a good starting point, the most illuminating follow-up question is: *"Which tests failed during the period, and which vulnerabilities remain unfixed today?"*

Under CPS 234, testing cannot be an occasional, informal exercise. It must follow a structured, systematic schedule run by independent, qualified security specialists who do not report to the people running the day-to-day IT systems. The overall testing plan must be formally reviewed at least once every twelve months, or immediately whenever significant changes are made to core banking infrastructure. Any control failure that cannot be remediated promptly must be reported directly to senior management and the board.

In August 2024, APRA cautioned against a frequent corporate habit: running identical, superficial tests on the same easy systems year after year while ignoring complex operational software ([APRA](https://www.apra.gov.au/news-and-publications/additional-insights-common-cyber-resilience-weaknesses), August 2024). Hiring an external tester once a year to test only the marketing website, while leaving core loan or deposit platforms unexamined, fails regulatory standards. Our practical overview of [penetration testing for Australian financial platforms](/blog/penetration-testing-financial-platforms) outlines how to rotate testing across web portals, mobile apps, and cloud networks.

A high-quality board pack provides a visual coverage map showing which critical platforms were tested over the past year, who conducted the work, and which identified weaknesses remain outstanding past their target remediation date.

## Backups the board has seen restored

![Rows of tape cartridges stacked floor to ceiling inside an automated tape library](tape-library.jpg)

*A secure data library stores electronic backup copies. Whether those copies can successfully restore banking operations during a crisis is the real test.*

It is easy for an organisation to assume that electronic backups are working quietly in the background. Proving that they can rebuild a shattered banking platform under emergency conditions is much harder. In APRA's initial study, more than one in three institutions had failed to test whether they could successfully restore critical systems from backup over the preceding twelve months. More than 20 per cent had never run a practice simulation of their cyber response plan.

APRA addressed this risk directly in June 2024 ([APRA](https://www.apra.gov.au/news-and-publications/security-and-adequacy-backups), June 2024). The regulator stressed that backup systems must be strictly separated and isolated from primary live systems. No single employee account or master password should possess the permission to modify or erase both live customer data and archived backups. If an intruder gains access to a primary server, the backups must remain untouchable.

For board directors, two straightforward questions cut through the technical noise:

- *"When did we last complete a full restoration of our core customer systems from backup copies, how many hours did it take, and did it meet our agreed recovery target?"*
- *"If an online criminal compromised a senior administrator's computer account, could they delete or encrypt our backup records as well?"*

The second question is the definitive test. It requires a clear, unambiguous answer rather than technical generalities.

## Who holds the keys

![Keys left in the door locks of an equipment cabinet's service panel](keys-in-panel.jpg)

*Brass keys in an office cabinet are straightforward to audit. Privileged digital administrative accounts across modern cloud systems require equal vigilance.*

APRA has issued repeated warnings regarding access permissions across financial platforms. In August 2024, the regulator highlighted that many institutions lacked a comprehensive, verified register of privileged accounts—the powerful administrative credentials capable of modifying core database rules, creating new user logins, or transferring funds.

In June 2025, APRA issued firm instructions to superannuation trustees: organisations must enforce multi-factor authentication (MFA)—a security mechanism requiring at least two independent checks, such as a master password paired with an encrypted mobile security prompt—across all administrative accounts and high-risk customer transactions. In superannuation and banking, high-risk actions include changing a customer's postal address or phone number, adding new withdrawal bank accounts, rolling over funds, or switching investment portfolios.

Although that directive was sent to superannuation trustees, the prudential standard applies with equal force to banks, credit unions, and insurance companies. Effective board questions include:

- *"Exactly how many privileged administrative accounts currently exist across our digital estate, and when did an independent manager last review that list?"*
- *"Which customer actions involving money movement or profile changes can still be completed with a simple password alone, without requiring an additional verification code?"*

## Incidents and the notification clocks

CPS 234 requires every regulated business to maintain robust, tested incident management plans for cyber disruptions that could reasonably occur. These response procedures must clearly spell out how critical incidents escalate to the board chair and chief executive. Furthermore, the plan must be formally rehearsed and reviewed at least once every twelve months.

When a severe security breach takes place, several strict legal notification clocks begin ticking simultaneously across different Australian regulatory authorities:

| Legal obligation | Who is bound | Mandatory reporting deadline |
|---|---|---|
| **CPS 234: Material security incident** | APRA-regulated financial entities | As soon as possible, and strictly no later than 72 hours after becoming aware |
| **CPS 234: Significant control breakdown** | APRA-regulated financial entities | No later than 10 business days after discovering a material weakness that cannot be promptly rectified |
| **CPS 230: Disruption to essential services** | APRA-regulated financial entities | No later than 24 hours after an operational outage breaches tolerable limits |
| **CPS 230: Major operational risk event** | APRA-regulated financial entities | No later than 72 hours after identifying an operational incident with material customer or financial impact |
| **Privacy Act: Notifiable Data Breaches** | Organisations governed by the Privacy Act | Notify the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) and affected individuals as soon as practicable when serious harm is likely |
| **Cyber Security Act 2024: Ransomware payments** | Australian businesses with annual turnover exceeding $3 million | Report any ransomware or cyber extortion payment to the Australian Government within 72 hours of payment |

The prudential deadlines are set directly by APRA's formal standards ([APRA, CPS 234](https://www.apra.gov.au/standards/cps-234); [APRA, CPS 230](https://www.apra.gov.au/standards/cps-230)). The mandatory ransomware reporting requirements have been in active effect since 30 May 2025 under Commonwealth law ([Home Affairs](https://www.homeaffairs.gov.au/cyber-security-subsite/files/factsheet-ransomware-payment-reporting.pdf)).

Cyber security incidents are no longer rare occurrences in Australian commerce. The OAIC recorded 1,205 formal data breach notifications during 2025—the highest annual volume since the mandatory reporting scheme began in 2018. Financial services accounted for 157 major breaches, representing the second most targeted sector in the country after healthcare ([OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/data-breach-notifications-increase-to-all-time-high-in-2025,-new-ndb-stats-show), July 2026).

A practical question for directors to ask executive leadership is: *"Who on our executive team is empowered to decide that a cyber incident is 'material', against what objective criteria, and has our team ever successfully run an unannounced drill proving we can notify APRA within 72 hours?"*

## The board's question list, with evidence

To assist directors during upcoming audit and risk committee meetings, the following table pairs core governance questions with the specific documentary evidence that management should produce:

| Core governance question | What a reassuring response includes | Documentary evidence to request |
|---|---|---|
| **Which information assets are critical or sensitive?** | A complete, actively maintained register that includes external cloud providers and third-party software tools | Approved asset inventory extract, formal classification guidelines, and confirmation of the most recent annual review |
| **How do we verify our technology suppliers' security?** | Documented, independent security audits of high-risk external vendors rather than passive self-assessment forms | Independent audit reports, external certifications, and signed contracts with mandatory breach notification clauses |
| **What proportion of our security controls is independently tested?** | A structured annual testing schedule covering core banking and payment systems, executed by independent specialists | Comprehensive testing schedule, system coverage matrix, and a register of open vulnerabilities past remediation deadlines |
| **Could the organisation recover from a ransomware attack?** | Proven, timed data restoration drills with electronic backup vaults physically and logically isolated from regular staff accounts | Written records of recent restore exercises, recovery time measurements, and an audit of administrator permissions |
| **Who possesses master administrative access?** | A strictly verified list of privileged user accounts with mandatory multi-factor authentication enforced on all high-risk actions | Up-to-date administrator register, multi-factor authentication coverage reports, and privileged access review logs |
| **Can we meet mandatory 72-hour reporting deadlines?** | Objective criteria defining material incidents, paired with tested escalation protocols connecting technical staff directly to the board | Incident simulation logs, minutes of cyber crisis exercises, and copies of any historical regulatory notices submitted to APRA |

If producing this evidence requires weeks of manual paper-shuffling and retrospective report writing, the organisation's controls are likely not functioning smoothly in everyday practice. Verifiable security logs generated automatically by live operating systems provide far greater assurance than slide decks assembled specifically for committee meetings.

Independent assessors look for this same operational discipline when evaluating organisations for [ISO 27001 certification](/blog/iso-27001-certification-australia-cost), meaning that diligent preparation for APRA oversight directly strengthens international security standards.

## What changes in 2026-27

APRA's latest corporate strategy signals heightened scrutiny across the financial sector: licensed institutions should expect more frequent, in-depth supervisory reviews focusing on cyber preparedness and emerging artificial intelligence risks ([APRA Corporate Plan 2026-27](https://www.apra.gov.au/apra-corporate-plan-2026-27/our-strategic-objectives), August 2026). In June 2026, APRA, the Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC), and the Australian Signals Directorate (ASD) convened joint briefing sessions with industry executives regarding modern cyber threats.

APRA expects cyber defenses to adapt continuously as fraudulent tactics evolve. Furthermore, the regulator has endorsed national guidance from the Australian Signals Directorate recommending that financial institutions develop transition roadmaps for post-quantum cryptography—advanced mathematical encryption designed to withstand future quantum computers—before the close of 2026, with implementation starting by 2028.

This introduces two forward-looking questions for board directors: *"How is our executive team updating our defense assumptions to counter automated, AI-driven phishing and identity fraud?"* and *"Do we have a multi-year plan to upgrade our data encryption to post-quantum standards, starting with our most critical long-term customer records?"*

## Common questions

### Does CPS 234 apply directly to our external software company?

No. The standard directly binds APRA-regulated financial institutions, not software vendors. However, the regulated institution has an unavoidable legal duty to assess the vendor's safeguards, review its technical testing, and enforce rigorous incident reporting in supply contracts. An external software partner unable or unwilling to provide this transparency places the regulated institution at serious compliance risk.

### Is an ISO 27001 certificate or SOC 2 report sufficient to satisfy CPS 234?

While valuable, an external certificate alone is rarely sufficient. Both frameworks reflect a specific scope defined by the vendor itself. The regulated institution's board must still confirm that the vendor's audited scope actually covers the specific customer data and payment functions being outsourced. Independent review of vendor controls forms a critical pillar of wider [technical due diligence for software development](/blog/technical-due-diligence-build-team).

### Who carries personal legal accountability under CPS 234?

Ultimate governance accountability rests squarely with the board of directors. In addition, APRA requires institutions to identify specific senior executives as accountable persons for information security under the Financial Accountability Regime (FAR)—a Commonwealth framework holding individual banking, insurance, and superannuation executives personally responsible for regulatory compliance. Boards should confirm that their internal FAR management map clearly defines this executive responsibility.

## Before your next board meeting

Prior to the next board or risk committee meeting, directors can test their organisation's practical readiness by selecting one high-value customer database—ideally one managed by an external software partner—and requesting four specific items from management:

- Its formal sensitivity classification and the exact date it was last reviewed
- The most recent independent security assessment of the external vendor's systems
- The signed outcome of the last full backup restoration test, comparing actual recovery time against approved limits
- A current list of every administrative user account authorised to access that system

When these four documents can be produced quickly and align consistently, the board can have genuine confidence in management's cyber reporting. When requests reveal gaps, confusion, or delays, the underlying weaknesses almost always stem from outdated software architectures, manual processes, or weak vendor contracts.
